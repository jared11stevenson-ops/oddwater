# Inspiration notes: *Se7en* (1995, dir. David Fincher, written by Andrew Kevin Walker)

Notes for the future. We take the *shape* and the *craft lessons*, never the plot, the lines or the killer's specific scheme. Everything here is meant to be re-expressed through Little Mercy's own lore (the lake, the Hush, 4:12, the old jury).

## What makes it work

1. **A theme that organises the murders.** Seven deadly sins give the killer a calendar, a reason for each victim, and the audience a way to predict (and dread) what comes next. Each death is a *sermon*, not just a kill. The detectives are always one step behind the lesson.
   - *For us:* serial killers already have a signature and a thread linking victims (the old photograph). A future "arc killer" can have a *structure* the detective can learn: a count, an order, a rule. Working out the rule is the mid-game turn, and then the dread is knowing what comes next and being unable to stop it in time.
2. **Two detectives, two worldviews.** Somerset is weary, cultured, a month from retirement and convinced the world is lost. Mills is young, angry, certain he can fix things. The case tests each one against the other's creed. The ending is a verdict on both.
   - *For us:* the lone detective can still carry the argument inside: stress, reputation and the Gallery of convicted suspects are our version of "the world is wearing you down". A future deputy/partner or a mentor figure (the retiring Sheriff) could play Somerset; the player is Mills.
3. **The city as a character.** Constant rain, no named city, grime, noise, rooms lit by one lamp. Nothing is clean. The environment makes the violence feel inevitable rather than shocking.
   - *For us:* Little Mercy's inversion: a *friendly* town that smells of toast, where the horror is in the ordinary kitchen. The contrast is our tone. Weather, fog and the lake should press on the scenes (use rain/fog days for the bleakest scenes).
4. **Showing the aftermath, not the act.** The film shows scenes *after*: the body, the staging, the one wrong detail. Our minds supply the rest, which is worse. Violence on screen is rare.
   - *For us:* already our rule: the player arrives after. Keep "the room is wrong" details (see `unease` in the casebook) specific, domestic and quiet.
5. **Procedure with patience.** Long scenes of reading, looking, cross-referencing. The library scene: two detectives searching books for the pattern while the killer is obviously ahead of them. Research is tense because it is the only thing they can do.
   - *For us:* the notebook, the lab queue, the camera footage. A "research" action (library, newspaper archive, records office) that turns scattered facts into a pattern could be the serial-killer mid-game verb.
6. **The killer is absent, then present on his own terms.** For most of the film we never see him. We know him through his work, his apartment, his notebooks (hundreds of them, in a precise hand). When he appears, he *turns himself in*, changing the question from "who?" to "what is he going to do?".
   - *For us:* a case where the identity is known early and the horror is the plan still unfolding; the detective cannot arrest their way out. Future "reverse" case type: you know who; you do not know what is coming, or what it will cost to stop.
7. **The killer's home is a horror set.** The apartment of notebooks, jars, years of method. We learn his mind from the *volume* of his preparation more than from any speech.
   - *For us:* a killer's home as a searchable location with a warrant: shelves of notebooks that reveal how long they have been watching the victims. The more you read, the more stress.
8. **The cruelty of timing.** The detectives arrive just late, again and again. Missing by hours is the film's engine.
   - *For us:* the pressure meter and the strike schedule do this mechanically. Tune so the player *almost* gets there: near-misses with a fresh scene, a victim found still warm, a suspect who left the house minutes ago.
9. **The ending is a choice, not a puzzle.** The final scene is not solved by cleverness. It is a moral trap: the killer has already won if the detective's anger is used as the last tool. The question is what you will do when it is offered to you.
   - *For us:* a final-act decision in a serial case where the "legally clean" move and the "satisfying" move diverge. The reputational and Gallery consequences should be felt long afterwards. No right answer; every answer costs.
10. **Hope without sentiment.** The last line argues the world is worth fighting for, and that you must carry on. A single sentence of defiance against everything the film has shown.
    - *For us:* the town can be worth saving. Even on the bleakest case the Courier's letters, the newcomers moving in and the chapel bell ringing at noon are the counterweight. Keep a thread of warmth alive: that is what the horror measures itself against.

## Craft lessons to apply

- **Make the pattern discoverable.** Give the player enough fair clues that a smart reader sees the rule *just before* the story admits it.
- **Stage scenes as arguments.** Each murder scene says something about the victim; the detective's job is to hear the sentence.
- **One unforgettable ugly detail per scene**, not many. We do this with the signature.
- **Let the detective be wrong in a way that costs something.** (Our wrongful conviction path.)
- **Silence is a weapon.** Long beats of just walking a dark house, no music, a clock, a drip. The mobile build should allow a "quiet mode" for scenes.
- **A killer with a *philosophy*, not just a motive.** Even our procedural killers should have a worldview a detective can argue with. Interrogation of a serial killer should be a conversation about *why the town deserved it*, and it should half work on the player.

## Ideas to bank (do not build yet)

- A **sin / virtue / ordeal** scheme for one authored multi-case arc, keyed to the town's real history, so the pattern is felt before it is spoken.
- A **mentor** character: the retiring Sheriff, old, tired, right about most things.
- A **research room** (library / newspaper morgue) verb for pattern-finding.
- A **killer's den** location type.
- The **moral trap finale** for authored serial arcs.
- **Weather as scene tone** (rain/fog days weight bleak scenes).
- **"You knew all along"** structure: a case in which the clues you ignored early are the ones that mattered.
